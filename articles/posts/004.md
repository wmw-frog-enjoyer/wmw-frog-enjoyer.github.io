# The Evolution of Mapping: Old Gens vs. Newgens

I feel like a lot of old players can see a clear difference in what is being mapped nowadays in comparison to what was being mapped a couple of years ago. With a somewhat recent boom in popularity, a lot of new players came in, and I feel like the more common mapping styles have changed quite a lot.

## What kinds of patterns were popular years ago, and what are the "meta" patterns today?

I have already talked a bit in a previous article about how stream slop was a new trend coming up due to how the ranking of maps is done on Rhythia Steam. That trend is definitely the most impactful on what I would call "newgen mapping."

A couple of years ago, a generalized ranking system was just not a thing, and Steam Rhythia wasn't a thing either. All we had was feedback on what the players liked to play and what mappers liked to map. I feel like ever since a rating system decided which maps had value or not, we started seeing a very big change in how mappers were mapping.

Before this, there were a lot of what nowadays are considered "generic patterns"—the type of mapping that isn't flashy but is very fun to play and represents the songs in a very straightforward way. Take BPM=RT or Flamewall; those are all very straightforward maps that don't really do anything flashy but represent the songs pretty well.

On the newer maps side of things, it gets a little bit trickier, and I'd like to separate it into two categories.

- **Category Number 1:** This consists of the old mappers from that time who have now grown bored when it comes to mapping generic things. I have seen a lot of old mappers start doing more generic patterns recently, but only a couple of months ago, it seemed like players were always complaining about how the new songs added to the game were way too techy and how they were not fun to play. What I heard from the mapper side is that when they spend years mapping the same generic patterns in the same very straightforward way of representing songs, it can get quite boring. As they learn how to map things better and how to represent songs differently, they tend to migrate towards patterns that give mappers more freedom of expression, like tech maps or quantums.
    
- **Category Number 2:** This includes the new mappers who are being judged based on a completely different basis. Most of these new mappers not only map Sound Space customs, but they also map for Rhythia Steam, or at least it does feel like they are heavily influenced by that mapping style.
    

Since there is a rating system on Rhythia Steam that judges how hard a map is to give RP to the players, players tend to play the harder maps more, and mappers also tend to map them more. When the rating system is so bad that only one type of pattern actually gives you RP, it seems like everyone wants to map and play that exact pattern.

This system migrated the decision power from what players like to play to how the rating system values different patterns, and that change has clearly favored stream maps. Maps became increasingly more generic, trying to stack as many streams as possible into a single song. There was no longer a need to mix patterns or to think about how fun a map is to play; as long as the map had a lot of streams, it gave a lot of RP, and that was the very thing that made the stream slop epidemic as bad as it nowadays is. The new mappers seem to have lost their touch with what players want to play, and the new players also seem lost, trying to get the most amount of RP instead of actually looking for good mapping. Since newer players aren't tired of stream slop because of how short of a time they have been playing the game, it makes it so that the same epidemic becomes even worse.

## How did old mappers choose to represent a song compared to how newgens do it?

This is a bit more on the personal side of things, as I am not a mapper myself, but I do feel like there is a pretty big difference in the mapping philosophies from the old days when compared to now.

Before, I feel like most mappers wanted to have fun mapping, wanting to make things that would fit the songs well and, over all of that, would be fun things to play. They didn't rush maps one after another; they took enough time to make the maps fun enough and good enough. Of course, there was a fair share of undercooked or overcooked maps, but the general philosophy was making something nice to play that represented the song well.

Nowadays, I feel like there is a clash between two different and almost entirely opposite sides of the spectrum. On one side of the spectrum are the mappers who make stream slop, who are playing for the system and letting a bad system decide how worthy their maps are to be played. They try to pump out as many maps as possible, making them simpler and simpler, and making them simply less fun to play.

On the other hand, there are the mappers who seem to have grown with the editor and are now representing songs in the most creative way—in a way that is very fun to watch and map, but so techy and hard to play that players no longer like them. It is not that they are poorly mapped; it is that they are too techy, and most players don't like spending time trying to understand the complex tech patterns the mapper used to represent the song.

## How have the tools used to discover maps changed?

Another thing that has changed a lot over the years is how players find new maps. For a good while, new maps were hidden on spreadsheets that different people made with the codes that they knew. Since official updates only happened once every three months, if not more, players didn't really expect much from the in-game maps. I remember waiting seven months for an update at some point, and when it finally happened, I didn't like any of the new songs added.

On the other end, we did have the Dive Challenges spreadsheet for SSPM maps, but those could only be played on nightly builds, though most people who cared about maps were on nightly anyway since updates on Sound Space were so far apart and the configuration options were limited.

As time went on, we did get an official spreadsheet with most of the customs—a place where people could actually submit maps—but there still wasn't a sorting system until very recently.

Very recently, Steam Rhythia came out, and with it, a whole website dedicated to hosting SSPM files, showing the star rating for the maps, and even showing a preview of them. This new interface allowed players to sort the maps they wanted based on the amount of stars they had. If anything I have said about the rating system stuck with you, you know the highest-rated maps that are the easiest to pass are stream slop.

On the other front, we have a new website for Sound Space custom maps too, which introduced a lot of different filters. The one that I think is the most important is the popularity filter. The website tracked the amount of times a song was copied, and it could separate the songs in order of popularity. But since the customs website uses the same rating system as Steam Rhythia, and a lot of newgen mappers making customs are also making maps for Steam Rhythia, the customs website became a place full of stream slop, and a place where most of the most popular maps are streams.

## Conclusion

I think there are many factors that have driven mappers to map the way they nowadays do, but I think the biggest culprit for why we see so much slop can be easily pinpointed back to the rating system introduced by Steam Rhythia. It migrated the incentive of mapping from having fun and making playable, enjoyable maps to "how many stars can I get on my map while making it the easiest to pass."
