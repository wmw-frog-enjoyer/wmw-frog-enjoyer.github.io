# The Moving Goalpost: How Skill Progression Alters the Player Experience

Remember when you passed your first brr? It must have felt so good. Your heart was racing, you probably clipped it, maybe showed it to your friends. Now imagine doing that same pass today. You probably wouldn't even save the replay.

From then to now, what has changed? Did the maps get worse? Did you stop enjoying the game? Or did your brain just adapt?

That problem is called the hedonic treadmill.

## The Psychology behind it

In psychology, the hedonic treadmill is a widely studied phenomenon that happens when someone gets used to a certain level of discomfort or accomplishment, establishing it as their new baseline for how things should go.

For example, when you buy a new phone, you can instantly notice how much faster it is compared to your older one. Maybe you notice a better camera, or better battery life. Whatever it is, it is clear to you that the new phone is better than the old one.

And yet, after a week goes by, it seems like you have been using that phone for a year, and all of the upgrades that once seemed so grand are now just the norm.

That happens because, after enough exposure to a certain level of performance from the phone, you simply grow used to it. We as humans adapt to the events that surround us, both the good and the bad. Meaning that, at some point or another, if nothing changes, it will just become normal.

## The Hedonic Treadmill in Sound Space

Every rhythm game focuses around progression, and Sound Space isn't any different. There are different difficulties for songs, with different star ratings, and modifiers that can make your life even harder. The better you become, the more you progress in terms of which maps you play and which modifiers you use.

But when a game surrounds itself so much around accomplishment, it also becomes prone to the hedonic treadmill.

At first, you want to pass Nightune, then your first brr, then you want to pass a 15-star, and at some point you start asking yourself why you can't get a sub-20 pass on this 13-star speed plus plus plus.

In every single step of that journey, every time you hit a goalpost, you do feel accomplished. What I am questioning isn't the feeling of accomplishment, but the trigger for it, and that trigger is always changing as you get used to different levels of accomplishment.

The first time you pass a brr, you feel moved. It isn't something that you just do. The second time you pass a brr, you feel very happy that you can repeat the result, and that it wasn't a one-time thing. The third time, you are already used to the map. The fourth time, you don't even care that much anymore, and by the tenth time, you already want a bigger challenge.

## The Score Paradox

Imagine two players. Player A gets their first 95%, and Player B gets a 98.9% on the same map. Objectively, Player B played better, but Player A is probably much happier. Why?

That is because the sense of accomplishment isn't based on what you actually did. Instead, it is based on what you expected to do. While Player A expected to pass the map and successfully accomplished it, Player B wanted to get a 99% but failed.

It isn't about your objective performance; it's about what you were going for in the first place.

## Difficulty Inflation

As you improve, the maps that you thought were impossible become your warmup. The people you looked up to aren't as far away from your level as they once were.

I remember that I thought playing BPM=RT S+ was impossible, and I mentioned in another article that I played that map after months without touching it. My first instinct was doing it S+, since that is nowadays the speed I play this map at, and once I finished it, I was like: "Did I really think this was impossible?"

It was surreal to me that a map that nowadays doesn't require much from me was one of the maps I looked at and said, "I will never pass that."

In that sense, I believe that this is quite the process. I once thought a medium map was impossible, and at some point they became quite easy. When they became quite easy, I thought that Nightune was the new impossible map. When I did that too, I thought that Insane Logic was an impossible pass.

I don't think I have ever made a pass I considered impossible, not because to this day I haven't been able to pass a medium map, but because as my skill grew closer to the level of those impossible maps, my definition of those impossible maps had also changed.

The expectations of having maps I thought were impossible and maps I thought were easy never really changed. Throughout all of my Sound Space journey, I have always considered some maps easy and some maps hard, but as my skill moved, those maps I considered easy or hard would also change.

And that phenomenon is what I think is fascinating.

## Leaderboards

I think this is something that goes for every single rhythm game, as almost all of them have leaderboards per song. When you play a map for the first time, you are likely not going to land in a super high spot. Let's say you passed a brr and landed in the top 300 of the song, and then you set the objective of getting top 200. Once you hit that, you want top 100, then top 50, and then top 10.

You stop actually enjoying the game and feeling the song and the map, and you start playing just to see and show numbers. As your goals go higher, your expectations for what you should and shouldn't get change.

While before you were okay with anything, now you want and expect passes with 98% accuracy, and next you will want a pass with fewer than 10 misses. Your goal, your destination, your goalpost keeps moving.

## Why new content feels exciting

I think a lot of players are shifting from playing mainly the in-game songs to playing customs. There is almost an infinite amount of customs available to the player. In comparison, there are only a couple of new songs added weekly, maybe three or four.

When you play something completely different, you don't quite have any expectation for the map. It's the first time you try it, and it temporarily resets that adaptation. Of course, that reset isn't that big considering you still have the star ratings, which create an expectation, but it still reduces the amount of it.

And yet, even those maps become normal too.

A lot of people say that the game was way more fun before, and now all the new maps suck, all the current maps suck, and everything is terrible. But I think what really changed wasn't the game; it was themselves.

They have simply adapted to the game, and what once was so exciting and new is now simply normal.

## Burnout

But the treadmill is exhausting. Your improvement slows down over time, but your expectations don't. When your progress slows down and the gap between what you can do and what you think you should be able to do grows, so does your frustration.

A lot of people think they are getting worse or losing their ability to evolve, when in reality progress just becomes slower at some point, and that is inevitable.

### The reward-to-effort ratio

When you first get into Sound Space, it feels amazing passing each new map. Every single pass is a new accomplishment. You can beat your goals with the game rather easily, and you improve rather quickly.

You spend a couple of hours to get a pass on the next map you are aiming for, maybe a couple of weeks before your first Logic, and maybe a month before your first brr, but those accomplishments are still in somewhat of a quick succession.

But as time goes on, the improvement of passing a whole different grade of map just doesn't exist. Your goals shift from playing a harder difficulty of map to playing an extra speed, and as you move further down the line, those objectives shrink more and more.

You start spending months trying to go from 97% to 98%. The same amount of time you used to pass a whole different class of map is now being used to get 1% better.

You are still improving, at least objectively speaking, but when you actually look at the time you seem to have wasted on such a simple goal, it's normal to feel bad about it. The effort required to achieve those goals grows, while the goals themselves and the mental reward of accomplishing them shrink drastically.

### The death of "fun" maps

At first, you are super pumped about passing Insane for the first time. But then you do it again, and maybe 10 more times after that, and all of a sudden that map is too easy for you and you no longer have fun playing it.

You just lost a part of the game. You just lost something you enjoyed doing. You ultimately start minimizing your enjoyment of the game, seeing the map pool you actually consider fun shrink rather than expand.

You get more picky about song representation, patterns, speeds, and mappers. The songs you once thought were super fun no longer are, and the ones that do meet the requirements are now too easy for you to keep playing them.

And all of a sudden, the game doesn't look so fun to play anymore, not because it is any different, but because your relationship with the game has fundamentally changed.

### Grinding vs playing

At some point, you stopped caring about having fun, and now you simply want to improve. You chase down metrics instead of enjoying the process.

The motivation changes from something you simply enjoyed to something you need to show others, because you need to show other people that you can pass a certain song at a certain speed, with a certain accuracy. You need to show yourself that you can do that.

And when you stop playing the game and start working on it, it stops being fun to play, and that is where burnout happens.

## Comparison

The treadmill becomes worse with social media. When you get into a Discord server, you talk to people in the community, you look at their scores, you want to be like them, you want to pass the same maps they can.

You no longer compare yourself to who is on your server, or even people who are in a similar skill range to you. Now you are comparing yourself with the best players in the world.

When you stop thinking about your gameplay alone and start comparing yourself with people who have such a big gap in skill compared to you, you start feeling like you are falling behind. You start thinking that you are not improving enough.

## Why players quit

The majority of people don't quit the game because they got worse. I think you need to play quite a lot to actually get worse, and that is a feat that not many people have accomplished. Of course, you have bad days, and you will play badly for a month or two if you haven't touched the game in a while. But getting genuinely worse is something that is quite rare.

The truth is that the majority of people quit because they don't improve, or at least they aren't able to satisfy what they see as a proper rhythm for improvement.

Because they are lacking in comparison to their friends, because they spend so long trying to pass a map and they are yet to do it, or simply because there is nothing new and the gameplay doesn't feel exciting anymore.

When every improvement becomes smaller and requires exponentially more effort to accomplish, eventually the reward of actually accomplishing it won't be able to match all the time you spent.

## Can you escape it?

But knowing all of that, knowing that at some point the reward won't be enough, what can I do to escape that treadmill?

You don't escape the treadmill. That isn't how things work. You can simply slow it down. Stop measuring yourself by those objective metrics of what you can or can't pass, what accuracy you get on a certain map, or what people think of you, and start playing the game however you feel like playing.

Shift the motivation from the exterior of wanting to play better and get noticed by people, to something internal, like simply wanting to have fun, test out new maps, talk to the community, make new maps, or whatever other way you find to have fun.

Not because you achieve a concrete goal, but because the process itself is fun.

Replay old maps, celebrate improvements even if they are the smallest size, only compare yourself to yourself from the past, take breaks, play other games, live a life, touch grass.

The goal was never reaching the top. The goal is having fun playing a rhythm game you enjoy.
