# The Difference Between Good Maps and Bad Maps

## What is a slop?

Rather than trying to define a good map immediately, I want to look at the different aspects that contribute to one. Only after putting those pieces together can we arrive at a meaningful definition.

As I mentioned in my stream slops article, I believe a slop is a map where all of the drops consist of only one pattern, and the pattern has no variety across the map and similar ones. So basically, a repetitive map with only one pattern.

I think it is important to say that good maps are not one-trick ponies; they usually have a main pattern and a secondary one. The two fundamental patterns of the game are jumps and streams. Most good maps will use them even when they aren't the main focus, and then use more secondary patterns to make the drops more interesting.

## Over-commitment maps suck

I made an entire video dedicated to stream slops, and I think I raised a pretty interesting point in that video.

After looking back on old maps that were repetitive streams, I played bpm=rt again. I felt like I hadn't played it in years, though it's more likely that it had only been a couple of months. When I played it, I felt somewhat close to when I played a stream slop. I used to love that map, but now it was kinda bad.

I don't think bpm=rt is a bad map; it represents the song well and even has an interesting light vibro and some slides. For some reason, it felt somewhat close to the sensation of playing a stream slop.

I think that sensation comes from association. Since I have played so many stream slops already, I have started to associate heavy stream maps with bad maps. And while BPM=RT isn't a slop map, it is heavily based around its streams, and that makes the sensation of it similar to the one I have when playing stream slops.

The point that I am trying to bring up is that when you rely too hard on one pattern, even when it isn't a slop, it can sometimes, depending on the circumstances, feel like one.

This isn't to say that BPM=RT should have had more jumps. Some songs genuinely call for one dominant pattern. The problem begins when the pattern exists because the mapper relies on it, not because the song demands it.

## Density in mapping matters

When we talk about the fundamental patterns of jumps and streams, I think one of the main things that comes to mind is how they are different approaches to a problem. I think there are songs that are better represented with streams and songs that are better represented with jumps, and that choice can highly change the gameplay of a map.

I think that difference in gameplay comes not only from the obvious difference in the pattern chosen to represent the song, but also in how the secondary patterns bend themselves to complement the primary ones. That bend has a lot to do with how dense the mapper wants a map to be.

So in a low-density setting, for example, where you are making a jump slide, it is very unlikely you will be using a U-shaped slide. Instead, it is much more likely that you will use an I-shaped slide, as it better complements jumps.

If the secondary patterns don't complement the map correctly, they can make a map feel off or wrong, and so they are a big key to what I would consider makes a map good.

## Representation of the song

I think, more than anything, the most important thing over what any player might argue is important is how the song is being represented. I think that a song dictates how a map would go.

One very simple example of it is the BPM. Songs with very high BPMs usually have lots of stacked notes to properly represent those high BPMs. They usually tend to use streams, as they have more notes than jumps.

Meanwhile, songs that go slower usually need "tricks" to make them harder or more interesting, and they usually benefit from a jump or low-density pattern.

It's hard for me to put into words when a song should be a stream or when it should be a jump. It's just something that comes with experience as a player. Maybe mappers have figured out a way to categorically determine whether a song should be mapped on high density or low density, but I haven't really found a way to explain it in words.

It is interesting to me how the most important part of what makes or breaks a map is the part that I can explain the least. To better understand what a good representation looks like, I'd personally recommend playing good maps. You can ask more advanced players to recommend you things, or you can look through the Ansilian archives where there are good maps for you to play. Ultimately, I think the best way of learning which pattern should be where is by listening carefully to the song and associating it with the maps you have already played and seen.

## Mappable songs

I just mentioned how representing a song is so important, but I think it is important to make it very clear that one person can't map all songs. What people consider to be mappable is very different, but certain mappers simply can't map certain songs.

I think there are a couple of songs in Sound Space I wouldn't really touch if I was the one to map them—songs that I didn't consider were possible to map. Songs like "Dead Inside" or "Save You" aren't ones that I would consider mappable, and yet they are not only in the game but also are maps that I really enjoy playing.

Mapping is expressing the song, and when you can't quite picture how a map of that song should go, it is almost impossible to make a good map.

### Following the lyrics

Another thing I have noticed is that the maps I enjoy the most rarely place their main emphasis on the vocals. They might still acknowledge them, but the gameplay is usually built around the instrumental rhythm instead. I think this makes mapping much easier, because my first instinct is often to follow the lyrics, and that almost always results in a map that feels disconnected from the rest of the song.

In all the maps I have played, I don't think I have ever found one that primarily revolves around the vocals while still representing the song as well as maps built around the beat. Vocals are naturally less consistent than the instrumental foundation, which makes them much harder to use as the backbone of a map. The beat, on the other hand, provides a much more stable structure for gameplay.

That doesn't mean vocals should be ignored entirely. They can still be emphasized or used as accents throughout a map. However, from my experience, trying to make them the primary focus almost always leads to a worse result than letting the instrumental rhythm dictate the gameplay.

### Song picking in Sound Space is different

While most rhythm games have multiple things you need to do at once in order to beat them, in Sound Space, you only need to move your mouse. And it isn't like you need to move it super precisely, as the notes are pretty big, and the entire gameplay is set to a 3x3 grid that isn't too hard to move in. Of course, there is quantum that can make notes smaller, or off-grid that gives you a bit more space to play, but ultimately you are kinda stuck with quite an easy platform.

When the mechanics of the game are based on such an easy platform, you need to push the hard part of the game to the maps and songs, meaning that you have way less wiggle room to stretch a slow song into making it hard. For a song to make a good map, you need to have a clear vision of what a map should look like in Sound Space, and there simply aren't that many songs that fit.

While a lot of rhythm games don't have big songs because the community is small or because of copyright reasons, Sound Space suffers from another and arguably bigger issue. And that is that most people can't map most songs; there simply aren't that many mainstream songs people can map.



## Conclusion

When a map uses a main pattern it relies on and then complements it harmonically with other patterns, properly representing the song, emphasizing the things that need to be, with breaks that seem well put, without overmapping it, making it both playable and enjoyable—that is when I consider a map truly good.

And while that seems like a lot of requirements, most of them are met without needing much thought from the mapper's side. I think if you ask me in a couple of months what a map needs to do to be good, I probably won't answer exactly what I just wrote. I think I likely won't be able to answer it at all, actually, but I can never forget the feeling of finding a new map that strikes all the chords you wanted it to.
