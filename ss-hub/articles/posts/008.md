# The Beginner's Guide to Sound Space Settings

4 months ago I made my most popular video, a settings guide. And now, four months later, I see many issues with that video. I think the main issue was that I gave beginner advice and more advanced advice in the same video. While that is not too bad as I clearly labeled the sections, I still think it could mislead people into not doing things that they like just because it would give them a super small edge. And so in this video, I will explain settings in Sound Space for beginners.

## Settings Menu

First, to find the settings menu, walk to one of the arcades in the lobby, hold E to enter the arcade, follow the tutorial, and then go to the main menu of the arcade. There you will be able to find the settings option right above the exit button.

When you press the settings button, you will be greeted by quite the overwhelming menu, but I will break down what each option is.

Right in front of you, you have your settings block. That box is where we will mostly be working in this video, as it is the way to change all of your settings.

To the left side of the settings block, you will locate a vertical stack of multiple categories of settings. Clicking on different categories changes the settings block to the category chosen.

To the left of the vertical stack, you will locate the settings profile manager. I will teach you how to use it in a second. By the time I recorded my last video, this settings profile option was not in the game, and I think it can be a great tool for experiencing different settings and picking the one that is right for you.

To the right side of the settings block, you will locate the skin tab. This tab is responsible for customizing your skin and adding new skin profiles. I will teach you how to import a skin profile, but I will not teach you how to make skins yourself, though that can easily be learned by trial and error.

Right behind the settings block, you will be able to locate the skin preview. As the name suggests, it shows you a preview of what your skin will look like. It can help a lot when making a skin. As a side note, by the time I am recording this video, the colors of the preview don't fully match the ones you see in gameplay due to an issue with light levels on different menus. This may have already been fixed by the time you watch this, but if it isn't, the preview is still a great way of having some sort of reference for the general color of things.

### How to use the settings profile menu

- **To make a new profile**, simply click the blue button with a plus sign located in the bottom right corner of the profiles block.
    
- **To deploy a profile**, simply click on the green button located to the right of the profile name.
    
- **To delete a profile**, simply click on the red button with a trash can icon located to the right of the deploy button. Be careful, as there is no secondary prompt preventing you from misclicking and permanently deleting a profile. It is recommended that you save profiles both in game and out of game as a safer way to avoid losing them.
    
- **To overwrite a profile**, if you have changed the settings, there is no need to make a new profile and delete the old one. You can simply click on the blue button with the floppy disk icon located to the left of the profile to overwrite it. Note that when you press the overwrite button, you will get a pop up asking you if you are sure of this decision. If you are, simply press yes and the profile should overwrite.
    
- **To export your current configuration**, click on the yellow button with an arrow pointing down located in the bottom right corner of the screen. After you press the button, the data will be shown in the bottom center section of the block, where you are able to select, copy, and send it to other people.
    
- **To import data**, you first need to find a profile you want to copy. Two ways you can find a profile are by looking in the player profiles thread on the official Discord server of Sound Space Studio, or alternatively, you can go into the Ansilian Archive in the settings section and filter for the SS format. After copying the data, go back to Sound Space and locate the writable area on the profile block located at its bottom center. Click on it, paste the configuration profile you just copied, and then finally press the green button located in the bottom right corner of the block. You will receive a warning saying that your current settings will be overwritten, so it is recommended that you save your current settings first. If you are okay with your settings being overwritten, press yes, and now your settings should match the ones on the profile you just copied.
    

### How to use the skin manager

I will not be teaching how to make new skins, simply how to import and export them. To find the skin profiles, click on the green button saying manage profiles located in the bottom center part of the skins block.

- **To create, deploy, delete, or overwrite skin profiles**, you can simply follow the exact same steps used for the settings profile menu. Use the blue plus button to create a new profile, the green button to deploy it, the red trash can to delete it, or the blue floppy disk to overwrite it. Just remember to be careful since there is no confirmation prompt when deleting a profile.
    
- **To export your current skin**, click on the yellow button with an arrow pointing down, exactly as you would for settings.
    
- **To import skin data**, find a profile in the skin profiles thread on the official Discord server or in the Ansilian Archive under the ss skin format. Paste the copied data into the writable area of the profile block, press the green button, and confirm the overwrite prompt so your new skin can be applied.
    

## Generic settings

There is a pretty heavy overlap within some of the settings that good players use. I will not be going too deep into what each of them does, but I think it could be interesting for you to learn them later.

### Gameplay tab

On the gameplay tab, I recommend having note push back disabled and note fade enabled.

Note push back makes it so that the notes disappear behind the screen, which means it is more likely that you will be seeing more notes at once, making it harder to understand what is happening on your screen.

On the other hand, note fade makes the notes fade at the start of the hit window. The hit window is the window of time where if your mouse is on top of the note, it counts as a hit. Note fade not only allows all of the notes to disappear uniformly regardless of whether you hit them or not, but it also makes them disappear a little bit before you hit them, which allows you to clear your screen and more easily read patterns.

### Inputs tab

On the inputs tab, it is important to disable absolute mode, as it creates a specific area in which you can move, almost like changing your sensitivity, but if you use a mouse, that option should remain disabled. If you use a tablet or are on mobile, you can have absolute mode enabled and edit its area by clicking on the edit play area button.

I would also recommend disabling the enable pause section, as pausing songs is a bad habit that makes the game easier, but most people don't take scores seriously when you pause in the middle of a map because it gives you a very big advantage. I think it's important to make it clear that pausing in the middle of a map disqualifies you from getting onto a leaderboard, you cannot pause in Versus mode, and pausing leaves a permanent change on your stats page.

Disable the option to enable controller unless you are playing on a controller, otherwise, your cursor will be dragged to the center of the screen.

And finally, and probably most importantly on that tab, I would highly recommend having cursor drift enabled, as when disabled, it allows your mouse to go outside of the grid, and playing like that is quite miserable.

### GFX tab

On the GFX tab, I recommend that you disable everything that you can disable, and for the 3 bottom options, you can leave them as default or tweak them to your liking.

All of the options under the GFX tab make it so that there are more effects on your screen, and more effects on your screen make it harder to read and understand patterns.

## Important settings

Now I will explain the most important settings and the most relative ones. All of them can be found under the gameplay category.

### Approach rate

The first of them is the approach rate, which from now on I will refer to as AR, as it is the usual way people refer to it. The AR is responsible for determining 2 things in your settings: the speed of the notes and the space between notes.

As the AR increases, both the speed of the notes and the space between them also increase. This means that if you have a very high AR, the notes will be very far apart, which makes reading patterns much easier, but on the other hand, they will be very fast, which makes it harder for you to react in time to them.

On the other hand, if you have a low AR, the opposite happens. The notes will be very slow, so it is easy to react to all of them, but they are also closer together, which means that in denser songs it becomes harder for you to understand what is happening on your screen.

Later in the video, I will explain how to pick your AR correctly so you don't overdo it, but it is important to note that it is a personal decision and you should pick something you feel more comfortable playing, not something that I simply recommended to you.

### Approach distance

The second of the important settings is the AD, or approach distance. The AD dictates how far away notes spawn from your screen.

That one is simpler than the AR, as it only affects one thing. If you have a very high AD, the notes will spawn very far away from you, and if you have a very low AD, the notes will spawn very close to you.

Picking your AD is definitely the most important part of choosing your settings, as it is the foundation for what your other settings should look like. I would personally recommend looking at ADs between 20 and 70, and yes, that is a very big range, but you can try some values on the very low end and on the very high end, and then narrow it down from there.

### Fade in length

The fade in length applies a fading in effect to the notes that are spawning, which makes it easier to distinguish between notes that are far behind or close together. Making your fade in length higher can cause effects such as rapid fading or losing some of the artificial spacing in the configuration. Those concepts are more advanced, and I have already made a video about that.

Since I won't be explaining the things that can happen when you turn your fade in length high, I will simply recommend that you have it set to 0, as this simplifies things. In my previous settings guide video, I went a bit further into what turning this setting up implies, but in this video, I will just recommend leaving it at 0.

### Lock Parallax

In my last video, I went on a deep dive into parallax profiles, and I might even make a new video talking more in depth about them, but for now, all you need to know is that the lock parallax is responsible for how much your camera shakes.

Having your camera shake is something that a lot of beginner players dislike, but it can be very helpful to guide your cursor to the right position. A range of values I believe is good is between 10 and 20. I personally use 15, and I believe the majority of good players use something around that 15 mark, though other lock parallaxes could also be interesting.

Having your parallax too low makes it harder for you to see when your cursor is not doing what it should, since the parallax helps you see the movement of the cursor and regulate the flow of movement. On the other hand, having the parallax too high makes it so that you can't really read the pattern, as your camera is shaking too much.

### How to choose settings

Now that you understand how all of those important settings work, I will explain how you can choose the settings that fit you best.

### Multipliers

The first thing that you need to understand in order to pick settings is that settings are not based on absolute values, they are based on the ratio between your AR and AD.

If the ratio between my AR and AD is the same as someone else's, the screen time of notes will be the same, even when the values are different.

Since I think writing down ratios is lame, as I don't like putting the colon after the value, I just made up the term multiplier, which is the ratio of your AR in relation to your AD. Or in other terms: "How much do I need to multiply my AD value by in order to get my AR?"

So, for an example, if I have an AR of 115 and an AD of 50, my multiplier is 2.3. And if my AR is 46 and my AD is 20, my multiplier is also 2.3.

While those settings are drastically different, the screen time on both of them is the same. How fast a configuration is depends on the multiplier, not the raw numbers.

#### Screen time

If, instead of calculating the ratio, you would prefer to have the screen time in milliseconds, I have found a formula to calculate that:

The approach time in ms is $750 \times \frac{\text{AD}}{10 \times \text{AR}}$.

And so, using the same example as before, if my AR is 115 and my AD is 50, my screen time in milliseconds is approximately 32.61 ms. And if my AR is 46 and my AD is 20, my screen time will also be 32.61 ms.

This means that notes stay visible for the exact same amount of time based on the ratio between AR and AD, not based on absolute numbers.

### What to pick first?

Now that you understand the concept of multipliers and how to calculate the screen time of notes, I can finally give you concrete instructions on how to pick your settings.

The first step in picking your settings is finding an AD that you like. There are 2 main ways of doing that. The first is simply guessing ADs between 20 and 70 and narrowing it down to something you like. You can fine tune the AD later on, it doesn't need to be perfect. The second way is going through other people's profiles, choosing what you think looks good, and then adapting from there.

After picking your AD, you need to pick your multiplier. A normal multiplier is going to land between 2x and 2.6x. I have observed that beginners tend to like multipliers below 2, but even the lowest multiplier in the range I'd consider normal is too fast for them. If that is your case, I wouldn't recommend that you rush to the 2x multiplier, but instead push slowly toward it by raising your AR little by little. This is the only setting that I believe is worth changing if your comfort zone doesn't align with the normal range, as it directly impacts your ability to read denser patterns or harder maps. It is almost impossible to read hard maps with multipliers less than 2 or higher than 2.6, it isn't fully impossible, but it becomes way harder.

I personally recommend 2.3, as it is in the very middle of the range and has a good balance between speed and readability. After picking your multiplier, simply multiply your AD by your multiplier to get your AR.

So, if the AD I liked was 48 and I liked the multiplier 2.3, my AR would be 110. From there, I would make fine tune adjustments like lowering my AD to 47 or raising my AR to 112. Those fine tune adjustments will be based on your experience with the settings and what you believe should be changed.

## Conclusion

And that is pretty much everything you need to know to get your settings properly set up. I know it can be a bit overwhelming with all the numbers and ratios, but the most important takeaway here is that your settings should be built around what feels comfortable to you, not just what a top player is using.

Take your time experimenting with your AD and tweaking your multiplier. Remember to always save your profiles before you overwrite them with something new or try out a different skin. Once you find that sweet spot, you will definitely notice a difference in your readability, whether you are just trying to pass denser maps or you are pushing your limits on those crazy S+++ speed ratings.
