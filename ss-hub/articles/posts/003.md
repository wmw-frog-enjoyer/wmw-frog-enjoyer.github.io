# The Math vs. The Feel: Why Multipliers Don’t Tell the Whole Story

I have explained on my settings guide video about how multipliers work, but settings are quite complex and today I'm here to share a bit more about what I know specifically about the Multipliers.

So, it is very obvious that the multiplier tells you the amount of screen time a note is getting, not on a measurement of time, but as a placeholder value that can be compared to other settings. What I didn't say is that there are ways that the screen time of notes can be affected without changing your multiplier.

One very common way of changing the multiplier, making it faster, is by raising the fade-in length. This makes it so that the notes fade in at the back when they spawn, which makes the time where they are in full opacity smaller. And while that doesn't directly affect the screen time notes have, it does give the impression of using a higher multiplier, since you tend to notice less the notes that are further down the back.

Another very common way of raising the multiplier, and one that many people might not even realize it does, is by enabling note fade or half ghost. That setting makes it so that the notes fade out during the hit window period, which means they spend less time on screen. Similar to the fade-in length effect, the notes are spending the same amount of time on screen, but since they fade sooner, it makes it look like they are spending less time on screen than what they actually are, which gives the impression of a higher multiplier.

The last very common way of changing your multiplier without changing either the AR or AD is by enabling note pushback. That setting makes it so that notes only disappear behind the grid, which makes the notes stay on screen for a longer period of time, effectively lowering the multiplier.

You might think that enabling note fade or note pushback pose almost no change to the multiplier, and you would be right. Those settings affect a much more important thing than the multiplier and that is the feeling of hitting the notes, but I do think it is interesting that those settings can affect how you perceive the multiplier of a specific setting.

On the other hand, changing the fade-in length to affect the multiplier is a known way of keeping the settings as is but making them a little bit cleaner to read. People who use higher ADs tend to like having high fade-in lengths because the extra space makes it better to read notes on a lower opacity. Since the notes are spaced further away from each other, the effects of having a high fade-in length can help a lot in making reading easier because of the higher sensation of a multiplier, and yet not making non-massive patterns harder to read since you can still read the notes with lower opacity.

But players who use lower ADs tend to prefer to keep the fade-in length either low or zeroed. This is because when you don't have a lot of space to play with and you try fading the notes in, it makes the setting more prone to rapid fade, and it makes the feel of it much faster than what it would usually be.

On lower AD, it seems to be much harder to read things like jumps, even if you are using the same low multiplier as someone on a higher AD. Even if the multiplier dictates the amount of screen time a note has, it doesn't dictate the spacing between notes, since that is the AR's job. This means that even though the screen time is exactly the same, the spacing between notes being smaller and the notes being clustered closer together due to the lack of AD can easily cause confusion when doing complex patterns like jumps.

In those situations where the spacing makes things cluttered, using a dual-color mesh can help with reading patterns more than you'd think. Guiding yourself with the alternating colors can genuinely be a helpful thing to distinguish the rhythm. This is especially true when you use a lower AD, as the colors create a visual separation that the spacing itself fails to provide.

On the other hand, when you are facing a massive pattern like streams on lower ADs, the experience is completely different. These continuous patterns become noticeably easier to read. Because the notes are still moving pretty slow due to the low AR, you have more time to process the sequence. Furthermore, reading streams isn't inherently hard under these conditions since the pattern itself is very predictable, with very few mechanical changes from map to map. The condensed spacing actually keeps the entire stream neatly within your vision.

But when you talk about higher ADs, things basically flip. Because the spacing between notes is much higher due to the higher AR, and the artificial spacing is also higher because of the higher AD, it becomes much easier to differentiate between the notes that are closer and the ones further back. And when you are doing more massive patterns like streams, it becomes harder to follow them since, even though they have the same screen time as the ones with lower AD, they are still way faster than those, which makes it harder to react to streams.

How settings feel isn't as simple as knowing multipliers or knowing the effects of each setting like I just explained. While this knowledge can help you understand how to make settings better for your situation, it also means that there are more things that can go wrong when copying someone else's settings.

Settings are things that are highly personal. You can't just grab someone else's settings and expect them to fit you perfectly. It is normal to adapt people's settings, and it is also normal for you to need to adapt to the new settings yourself.

If your monitor is closer or further away from you, if your sens is lower or higher, if your mouse is lighter or heavier, if your monitor is big or small, with a low or high refresh rate, with a low or high brightness; these can all be things that affect your feel on playing a certain configuration. And while you might think that those things are too small to be noticeable, they do pile up. When they pile up enough, they start being the difference between something you are comfortable using and something that feels weird.

Adapting to settings and adapting settings to you should be things that you are constantly doing. There is no one-size-fits-all, and even the most experienced of players change their settings all the time. They don't change their settings because for however long they used their last settings they disliked it; they change it because they have changed, and so their settings need to change too.

When I take breaks from the game, even if they are 2- or 3-day breaks, when I come back to playing, my settings already feel weird. Adapting back onto my settings would take me longer and feel worse than just adapting what I feel like isn't working well for me.
